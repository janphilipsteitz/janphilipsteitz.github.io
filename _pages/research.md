## Job Market Paper

**Raise Fast, Cut Slow: Asymmetric Reversal of Recession-Driven State Tax
Increases**  
[PDF](/files/Steitz_RaiseFastCutSlow.pdf)

<details>
<summary>Abstract (click to expand)</summary>
<br>
Under balanced-budget rules a recession should force states to adjust their tax
rates, yet the most comprehensive study of state tax changes to date finds no
effect of recessions on rate levels. I document a clear short-run response
followed by faster and more frequent reversal, which narrows that puzzle without
dissolving it. Using a panel of 50 U.S. states from 1960 to 2022, I show that
recessions raise the probability of any major state tax increase by 7.7
percentage points while recoveries produce no offsetting cuts within three years.
A formal test rejects equality of the recession increase and decrease responses
at the 1% level, and rejects at the 5% level the equality of the recession
increase and recovery decrease coefficients. Those increases are then cut sooner
and more often, facing more than twice the hazard of a subsequent cut, and 28
percent survive a decade uncut against 62 percent of increases enacted in normal
times. Completeness differs at five years but not detectably at ten, and the
median increase of either kind still stands in full after a decade, so
substantial rate persistence remains. The asymmetry is clearest in the timing and
incidence of cuts rather than in how far they go. Separately, cumulative
recession exposure is not associated with higher rate levels. Together these
weigh against a permanent upward ratchet, though neither establishes that the
subsequent cuts account for the absence of accumulation. The contemporaneous
differential is a within-state gap between recession and other years, at 0.67
percentage points on the top income rate, rather than an accumulated stock.
</details>

## Working Papers

**Same Language, Same Market? Substitution Between Mexican and Other Hispanic
Immigrants in the United States**  
*Draft coming soon*

<details>
<summary>Abstract (click to expand)</summary>
<br>
Immigrants and natives with the same education and experience are imperfect
substitutes, and the leading explanation is that they differ in language and in
the tasks language allows. If so, two immigrant groups that share a language
should be far closer substitutes for each other than immigrants are for natives.
I test this prediction for Mexican and other Hispanic immigrants in the United
States in national education-experience cells over 1990 to 2024. Because the
shift-share instrument used for immigrant supply has no first stage for a single
origin group, I instrument Mexican supply with a demographic projection, cohorts
observed five to thirty-five years earlier aged forward with arrivals ignored.
The inverse elasticity of substitution between the two groups is 0.071 for
workers without a high school degree, 0.067 for high school graduates, 0.078 for
workers with some college, and 0.131 for college graduates, the first two
precisely estimated and the four not distinguishable from one another. The
implied elasticity of substitution is about 14, against 16 to 23 for immigrants
and natives in the same cells and the same design. Shared language does not make
immigrant groups one labor pool. National origin segments the market at every
education level about as much as nativity does, so a shared language is not
sufficient for two groups to be close substitutes, and language alone cannot
account for the immigrant-native wedge.
</details>

**Taxation after a Tax-Base Shock: Evidence from the Boll Weevil**  
*Draft coming soon*

<details>
<summary>Abstract (click to expand)</summary>
<br>
This paper asks how local governments adjust property taxation when an income
shock hits the industry on which their local economy depends. Such a shock can
reduce both property values and taxpayers' earning capacity. Governments can
ordinarily stabilize property-tax revenue by raising rates when assessed values
fall, but doing so preserves the tax bill faced by owners whose incomes have
also declined. I test whether that adjustment survives, using the boll weevil's
devastation of Southern cotton agriculture between 1892 and 1922. Using decadal
census data for 1890 to 1930, I compare counties of differing pre-weevil cotton
intensity within compact geographic regions. For a ten-percentage-point increase
in cotton intensity the levy falls by 0.119 log points. Full stabilization is the
restriction that the levy response is zero, and the baseline estimates reject it.
The assessed base falls as well, without an offsetting rate increase, and no
specification provides statistically significant evidence of one, the estimated
statutory-rate response being −0.031 log points. County-purpose estimates
indicate a decline of 0.150 log points in the levy counties set for themselves
and 0.069 in the rate. A fixed upper rate ceiling cannot mechanically explain
such a reduction. Pre-arrival patterns, sensitivity to cotton-specific calendar
paths, and a change in the levy definition across waves limit causal attribution.
The findings suggest that property-tax revenue stabilization can weaken where the
local economic base is concentrated.
</details>
